Actor guide

20 August 2026 · Raden Films

Why tracking your auditions matters:Not because you love spreadsheets.

Auditioning is the job. If you don’t write the jobs down, the season turns into a blur of emails, half-remembered casting names, and a vague sense that “something crime-y” happened in March.

A log won’t book you. It gives you something rarer in this industry: a clear picture of what’s actually happening — so you can prep smarter, talk to your agent with facts, and stop arguing with your own memory.

What a log is really for

You’re not collecting stamps. You’re answering a few boring questions that get expensive when you guess:

  • What’s due this week, and did the tape go out?
  • Which casting offices keep seeing you — and calling you back?
  • Are you mostly self-tape or in the room?
  • What’s your rough booking rate lately — not as a verdict on your talent, but as a snapshot of the pipeline?
  • What did you wear / choose last time, if they ask you back?

That last one gets overlooked. Callbacks often want the same energy you brought the first time. If you can’t remember the take, the wardrobe, or the note from the room, you’re reinventing a job you already did.

You’re not the only one saying this

Actors and coaches have been nagging each other about this for years. Worth reading in their own words:

Stage Milk — Why every actor needs an audition log is one of the clearest write-ups online: treat the business side seriously, track enough columns to spot patterns (who got you in, which offices, role size, progress), then actually look at the data instead of only feeling the quiet weeks.

William Mapother on record-keeping frames it as a marathon habit — meetings, roles, choices, even what you wore — so progress isn’t only “I booked” or “I didn’t.” Casting coach Marci Liroff pushes the same idea from the other side of the table: a diary (or spreadsheet) helps you recreate what worked at a callback, and gives a new agent real tools — fans in casting, rooms you’ve already been in — instead of vibes.

Cast Forward’s Actor Operating System treats the audition log as part of a wider “operating system”: performance journal + casting map + feedback archive. Separate that from a submission tracker if you self-submit a lot — one is “what I sent,” the other is “what I actually auditioned.”

Teacup of Wisdom is blunt about callbacks: you often need to do what you did before. Tracking is how you don’t drive yourself mad trying to remember.

Spreadsheets, templates, and people who film the setup

Plenty of actors start in Google Sheets or Excel. That’s fine. Petersen Voice Studio walks through why a searchable spreadsheet beats a paper notebook once the volume goes up. My Acting Agent on digital archives is less about the spreadsheet and more about where files live — year and project folders, consistent labels — so the log and the tape aren’t strangers.

What tracking won’t do

It won’t make casting fair. It won’t explain every “not this time.” A low booking rate can mean type, timing, or a season of near-misses — not that you should quit. Use the numbers as weather, not a verdict.

And if the log takes longer than the tape, you’ll abandon it. Keep fields light. Update the same day. That’s the whole game.
