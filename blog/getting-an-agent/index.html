Actor tips · Agent

19 August 2026 · Raden Films

Getting an agent:Treat it like a pitch, not a wish.

Agents are selective because they earn on your bookings. Outreach is a professional pitch: right people, clear materials, short emails, and a paper trail. This is the Getting an Agent list from Actor Tips — not a promise that one PDF gets you signed by Friday.

They get paid when you work

Typical commission is in the 10–20% range, which is why they do not take everyone who can cry on demand. In exchange they usually:

  • Help get you into rooms and onto tapes
  • Negotiate contracts and rates
  • Offer career and industry guidance
  • Manage professional relationships

If someone wants a big fee up front to “represent” you, that is a different conversation. Slow down.

Do not spray every office in the Spotlight directory

Target people who already work with talent at your stage — emerging lists, your region, your type of work (theatre, TV, commercials). Use IMDbPro and agency sites. Ask around in classes and reputable actor communities. A short, relevant list beats a hundred mismatched emails.

Make it easy to say yes

Have the kit ready before you write:

  • Current professional headshots
  • An acting CV that is actually up to date
  • A demo reel you would still stand by
  • Casting profiles and a simple online presence that match

Broken links and a 2019 reel are how you get deleted.

The product is still the acting

Classes, workshops, student films, community theatre, monologues, recording yourself and watching it back. Agents sell access. They cannot invent skill in the room. Keep training while you submit — it is not a side quest.

People remember how you follow up

Workshops, organisations, other actors, online spaces that are actually professional. Relationships open doors a cold PDF cannot. Follow up like an adult: once, with a reason, not a weekly “just bumping this.”

The email that does not waste their morning

Keep it short and clear:

  • A subject line a human can parse
  • Brief intro and a couple of real credits or training notes
  • Headshot and CV
  • Links to work, and a signature that looks like a person with a phone number

They are not marking your autobiography. They are scanning for “can I send this person out.”

The guidelines are the test

Read what that office asks for. Only contact agencies that are accepting submissions. Stay concise. If they say no attachments, do not attach. Respecting process is part of the pitch.

Walk in as a collaborator

If you get a meeting: dress like you take it seriously, arrive early, extra headshots and CVs, a plain-English version of the work you want, pieces ready if they ask, and you have actually looked up who they represent. You are interviewing them too.

Read the deal before you smile and sign

Know standard commission, what the agreement actually says, licensing if it comes up, and your rights as a client. Learn the red flags (upfront “guarantee” fees, vague exclusivity, pressure to decide in the corridor). If you do not understand a clause, do not pretend you do.

This takes longer than a week

Keep a record of who you wrote to and when. Update materials when the work changes. Follow up once. Keep training. Silence is normal; chasing daily is not.

A simple log of submissions sits next to your audition log — same brain, different column. Audition Tracker is built for the job list; keep agent outreach somewhere you will actually look. Measurements and finances in Toolkit are the boring extras that matter when a meeting turns into tax season.
