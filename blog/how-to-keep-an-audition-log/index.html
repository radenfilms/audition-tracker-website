How to keep an audition log (and actually stay organised)

If you are hunting for last week’s sides, guessing whether a tape went in, or telling an agent it was “something crime-y in March,” you are not disorganised. You are doing the job. Auditioning is the work. A log is how you stop that work living in five inboxes.

A decent tracker should give you three things: calm this week (what’s due, what’s pending), a clean file for each job, and a picture over months — booking rate, callbacks, the casting names you keep seeing.

A log is not a diary

Unless writing feelings helps you — fine. For most of us it is a record of what you submitted, when it was due, who it was for, and what happened next.

Coaches keep repeating this because it works. Once the season is busy, memory is not a system. The log fails when logging takes longer than filming — so keep it light.

Fields you’ll still fill in at midnight

Skip the 40-column template you will abandon. Track fields you will still fill in after a late-night tape.

Must-have:

  • Project title and role
  • Date of the audition or self-tape due date
  • Format: self-tape or in person
  • Casting office / casting director (as on the breakdown)
  • Status: pending, penciled, callback, booked, not booked

Worth it if you tape a lot:

  • Who got you in (agent, manager, self-submit)
  • Project type: TV, film, commercial, theatre, voice
  • Headshot, sides, and the tape on that same record
  • A one-line note: accent, slate, something you’d change next time

You do not need to log “who booked it” on day one. Add that later if you like pattern-hunting.

Spreadsheet, Notes, or an app?

People searching “audition log spreadsheet” are usually trying to get out of a pile of emails. That is a fair start.

A spreadsheet is enough if you open it the same day as every tape, you do not need reminders on your lock screen, and you are happy keeping files in Drive or Camera Roll separately.

Notes and screenshots fall apart once you have more than a handful of live jobs. You cannot sort by pending. You cannot see next week at a glance. You cannot answer “what’s my callback rate this year?” without counting by hand.

Deadlines do not live in your inbox

The fix is boring. It works.

  1. Create the record when the breakdown lands — before you storyboard the tape.
  2. Put the due date on a calendar the same minute. Not “I’ll remember.”
  3. Set a reminder the evening before, not five minutes before upload.
  4. Keep sides and the finished file on that job so you are not scrolling Camera Roll at 11pm.

If two tapes collide, the calendar is the truth. A list in email is not.

What a few months of jobs will tell you

The useful questions are simple:

  • How many auditions vs callbacks vs bookings (your booking rate)?
  • Are you mostly self-tape or in the room?
  • Which casting names keep coming back?
  • Are you always the “best friend” / “copper” / “mum in waiting room”? Useful for type — and for your reel.

That is career information, not a scoreboard of your worth. Quiet months happen. The log just stops you guessing.

If you do nothing else this week

  • Pick one system this week. Do not run Sheets and Notes and an app.
  • Add your last five jobs so the list does not feel empty.
  • Rule: no tape goes out until the job exists in the log with a date.
  • Sunday: two minutes — statuses, next week’s dues.

Logging, quickly

What is the best way to track auditions?

The one you update the day you tape. For many actors that starts as a spreadsheet and becomes an app once deadlines and files pile up.

What should I include in an audition tracker?

Project, role, date, format, casting, status, deadline. Attachments if you can. Everything else is optional.

How do I stay organised as an actor during a busy season?

Treat the log like call sheet admin: capture the job first, film second, update status when you hear back. Calendar for “what’s next.” Stats for later.
