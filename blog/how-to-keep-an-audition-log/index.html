How to keep an audition log (and actually stay organised)

If you search how to track auditions, keep a self-tape log, or replace a messy spreadsheet, you are not disorganised — you are doing the job. Auditioning is the work. A simple audition log is how you keep the work from living in five inboxes.

Why actors keep an audition log

Working actors do not lose jobs because they cannot act. They lose hours hunting for last week’s sides, guessing whether a tape went in, or telling an agent “I think it was for something crime-y in March.”

A log is not a diary of feelings (unless that helps you). It is a record of what you submitted, when it was due, who it was for, and what happened next. Coaches and career articles keep repeating the same idea because it works: once the season is busy, memory is not a system.

Three things a decent audition tracker should give you:

  • Calm this week — what is due, what is pending, what is already in.
  • A clean file for each job — project, role, status, tape, sides, reminder.
  • A picture over months — booking rate, callbacks, formats, casting names you keep seeing.

What to put in an audition log

Skip the 40-column template you will abandon. Track fields you will still fill in after a late-night tape.

Must-have

  • Project title and role
  • Date of the audition or self-tape due date
  • Format: self-tape or in person
  • Casting office / casting director (as on the breakdown)
  • Status: pending, penciled, callback, booked, not booked

Worth it if you tape a lot

  • Who got you in (agent, manager, self-submit)
  • Project type: TV, film, commercial, theatre, voice
  • Headshot, sides, and the tape attached to that same record
  • A one-line note: accent, slate, something you would change next time

You do not need to log “who booked it” on day one. Add that later if you like pattern-hunting. The log fails when logging takes longer than filming.

Audition spreadsheet, Notes, or an app?

People searching “audition log spreadsheet” or “Google Sheets audition tracker” are usually trying to get out of a pile of emails. That is a fair start.

A spreadsheet is enough if you open it the same day as every tape, you do not need reminders on your lock screen, and you are happy keeping files in Drive or Camera Roll separately.

Notes and screenshots fall apart once you have more than a handful of live jobs. You cannot sort by pending, you cannot see next week at a glance, and you cannot answer “what’s my callback rate this year?” without counting by hand.

How to track self-tape deadlines

“Never miss a self-tape deadline” is one of the most common reasons actors look for a tracker. The fix is boring and it works:

  1. Create the record when the breakdown lands, before you storyboard the tape.
  2. Put the due date on a calendar the same minute — phone calendar or the app calendar, not “I’ll remember.”
  3. Set a reminder the evening before, not five minutes before upload.
  4. Keep sides and the finished file on that job so you are not scrolling Camera Roll at 11pm.

If two tapes collide, the calendar is the truth. A list in email is not.

How to use the log (not just fill it in)

Once you have a few months of data, the useful questions are simple:

  • How many auditions vs callbacks vs bookings (your booking rate)?
  • Are you mostly self-tape or in the room?
  • Which casting names keep coming back?
  • Are you always the “best friend” / “copper” / “mum in waiting room” — useful for type and for your reel?

That is career information, not a scoreboard of your worth. Quiet months happen. The log just stops you guessing.

A 10-minute setup that sticks

  1. Pick one system this week. Do not run Sheets and Notes and an app.
  2. Add your last five jobs so the list does not feel empty.
  3. Rule: no tape goes out until the job exists in the log with a date.
  4. Sunday: two minutes — statuses, next week’s dues.

Quick answers

What is the best way to track auditions?

The best way is the one you update the day you tape. For many actors that starts as a spreadsheet and becomes an app once deadlines and files pile up.

What should I include in an audition tracker?

Project, role, date, format, casting, status, deadline. Attachments if you can. Everything else is optional.

How do I stay organised as an actor during a busy season?

Treat the log like call sheet admin: capture the job first, film second, update status when you hear back. Home/calendar for “what’s next”; stats for later.
