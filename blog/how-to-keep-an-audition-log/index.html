How to keep an audition log:Why it matters, what to write, and a system that sticks.

Auditioning is the job. If you don’t write the jobs down, the season turns into a blur of emails, half-remembered casting names, and a vague sense that “something crime-y” happened in March.

A log won’t book you. It gives you something rarer: a clear picture of what’s happening — so you can catch deadlines, talk to your agent with facts, and stop arguing with your own memory.

What a log is really for

You’re not collecting stamps. You’re answering a few boring questions that get expensive when you guess:

  • What’s due this week, and did the tape go out?
  • Which casting offices keep seeing you — and calling you back?
  • Are you mostly self-tape or in the room?
  • What’s your rough booking rate lately — weather on the pipeline, not a verdict on your talent?
  • What did you wear or choose last time, if they ask you back?

That last one gets overlooked. Callbacks often want the same energy you brought the first time. If you can’t remember the take, the wardrobe, or the note from the room, you’re reinventing a job you already did.

Keep it light. If logging takes longer than filming, you’ll abandon it.

You’re not the only one saying this

Actors and coaches have been nagging each other about this for years. Worth reading in their own words:

Stage Milk — Why every actor needs an audition log is one of the clearest write-ups online: take the business side seriously, track enough to spot patterns (who got you in, which offices, role size, progress), then actually look at the data instead of only feeling the quiet weeks.

William Mapother on record-keeping frames it as a marathon habit — meetings, roles, choices, even what you wore — so progress isn’t only “I booked” or “I didn’t.” Casting coach Marci Liroff pushes the same idea from the other side of the table: a diary (or spreadsheet) helps you recreate what worked at a callback, and gives a new agent real tools — fans in casting, rooms you’ve already been in — instead of vibes.

Cast Forward’s Actor Operating System treats the audition log as performance journal + casting map + feedback archive. Separate that from a submission tracker if you self-submit a lot — one is “what I sent,” the other is “what I actually auditioned.”

Teacup of Wisdom is blunt about callbacks: you often need to do what you did before. Tracking is how you don’t drive yourself mad trying to remember.

Owning IT Acting Studio (London) makes the same case from a working actor’s desk: a spreadsheet that covers production, casting, character, platform, tape vs room, callback, book, and who got it — so auditions stop feeling like self-tapes into the void and start showing patterns and relationships.

What to write down

Skip the 40-column template you’ll abandon. Only track what you’ll still fill in after a late-night tape.

Must-have:

  • Project title and role
  • Date of the audition or self-tape due date
  • Format: self-tape or in person
  • Casting office / casting director (as on the breakdown)
  • Status: pending, penciled, callback, booked, not booked

Worth it if you tape a lot:

  • Who got you in (agent, manager, self-submit)
  • Project type: TV, film, commercial, theatre, voice
  • Headshot, sides, and the tape on that same record
  • A one-line note: accent, slate, wardrobe, something you’d change next time

You don’t need “who booked it” on day one. Add that later if you like pattern-hunting.

Deadlines don’t live in your inbox

The fix is boring. It works.

  1. Create the record when the breakdown lands — before you storyboard the tape.
  2. Get the due date on a calendar the same minute. Not “I’ll remember.”
  3. Set a reminder the evening before, not five minutes before upload.
  4. Keep sides and the finished file on that job so you’re not scrolling Camera Roll at 11pm.

If two tapes collide, the calendar is the truth. A list in email is not. For folders and file names around the tape itself, see how to organise self-tape auditions.

Spreadsheet, Notes, or an app?

Plenty of actors start in Google Sheets or Excel. That’s fine. Petersen Voice Studio walks through why a searchable spreadsheet beats a paper notebook once the volume goes up. Demo Reels for Actors offers free Sheet templates for auditions, submissions, and contacts — useful if you want a blank structure tonight.

If you’d rather watch someone build it:

  • Acting Career Center — Keep Track of All Your Auditions with an Audition Log Spreadsheet — Curt walks through his free Sheet template (he’s tracked since 2009), what columns to start with, and how filters show bookings and casting offices once you have dozens of rows.
  • 2025 Audition Tracker (Acting Essentials) — a downloadable template walkthrough, including looking back over a year so a quiet week doesn’t rewrite your whole story.

My Acting Agent on digital archives is less about the spreadsheet and more about where files live — year and project folders, consistent labels — so the log and the tape aren’t strangers.

A spreadsheet is enough if you open it the same day as every tape, you don’t need lock-screen reminders, and you’re happy keeping files in Drive separately. Notes and screenshots fall apart once you have more than a handful of live jobs — you can’t sort by pending or see next week at a glance.

What a few months will tell you

The useful questions are simple:

  • How many auditions vs callbacks vs bookings (your booking rate)?
  • Are you mostly self-tape or in the room?
  • Which casting names keep coming back?
  • Do you keep getting “best friend,” “copper,” or “mum in the waiting room”? That’s useful for type — and for what belongs on your reel.

That’s career information, not a scoreboard of your worth. A low booking rate can mean type, timing, or a season of near-misses. Use the numbers as weather, not a verdict — more on that in actor booking rate explained. Quiet months happen. The log just stops you guessing.

That’s enough system

Pick one place. Add each job when the sides arrive. Don’t send a tape that isn’t on the list with a date. Sunday: two minutes for statuses and next week’s dues.

Start with a Sheet tonight if that’s what you’ll open. Or download Audition Tracker and keep it on your phone. Just start somewhere you’ll actually use again.

A few quick ones

What’s the best way to track auditions?

The one you update the day you tape. For many actors that starts as a spreadsheet and becomes an app once deadlines and files pile up.

What should I include?

Project, role, date, format, casting, status, deadline. Attachments if you can. Everything else is optional.

How do I stay organised in a busy season?

Capture the job first, film second, update status when you hear back. Calendar for what’s next. Stats for later.
