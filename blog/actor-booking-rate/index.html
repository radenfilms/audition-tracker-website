Actor booking rate explained:What the number means — and what it doesn’t.

Actors love to whisper about booking rates like they’re exam scores. They’re not. Used well, the percentage is weather on your pipeline. Used badly, it’s a reason to spiral after a quiet month.

Here’s the plain version: how to calculate it, how it sits next to callback rate, and when you should ignore it.

What booking rate actually is

Over a period you choose — a year, six months, this pilot season — take the number of jobs you booked and divide by the number of auditions you did. Multiply by 100 for a percentage.

Booked 3 of 30 auditions → 10%.

That’s it. No industry body publishes an official “good” number. Markets differ. So does how people count. Some actors only log self-tapes that went out. Some include chemistry reads and producer sessions as separate auditions. Decide your rules and stick to them, or the percentage becomes fiction.

Callback rate is the other half

Booking rate alone is blunt. Pair it with callback rate: callbacks ÷ auditions.

  • Few callbacks — materials, type, or submissions may be the bottleneck. Or you’re early in a new market.
  • Lots of callbacks, few bookings — you’re getting in the room (or the shortlist). The miss might be chemistry, availability, or simply not being the choice that day. Different problem than “nobody’s seeing me.”
  • Steady bookings without many callbacks — happens on smaller roles and commercials where the first tape is the decision.

Looking at both stops you “fixing” the wrong thing.

There is no magic good rate

Someone posting “I book 40%” might be counting differently, working a different market, or only sharing a hot streak. Series regulars, commercials, theatre, and under-fives don’t behave the same.

What matters more:

  • Is this year clearer than last year — or just louder in your head?
  • Are callbacks climbing even if bookings are flat?
  • Which offices keep bringing you back?
  • Are you mostly co-star / guest / commercial — and does that match what you want?

Compare yourself to your own log, not a stranger’s highlight reel. When you’re ready to dig past the headline percentage, see how to read your audition stats.

When the number lies

Small samples. Two bookings out of eight auditions looks like 25%. One more week of tapes and it’s dust. Wait until you have a real pile — dozens, not a handful — before treating the percentage as meaningful.

Pending jobs. If half your sheet is still “waiting to hear,” don’t calculate yet. Update statuses, then count.

Quiet seasons. August and Christmas can flatten the pipeline. A low quarter isn’t a character flaw.

Wrong denominator. Counting every submission on Casting Networks as an “audition” will crush the rate. Count things you actually taped or went in for — unless you’ve decided submissions are your metric, in which case label it a submission rate and don’t mix the two.

How to use it without spiralling

Glance at booking rate and callback rate every few months — not every Sunday night after a rejection email. Ask one practical question: what would I change if this trend held?

Examples:

  • Callbacks up, bookings flat → work the second round harder; keep notes so you can recreate the first take.
  • Always the same type, never booking → talk to your agent about submissions, or widen the reel.
  • Agent-sourced tapes book more than self-submits → useful for where you spend evenings.

None of that requires a perfect spreadsheet. It requires statuses you actually update. That’s why keeping an audition log comes first — the rate is just maths on top.

A few quick ones

What is an actor’s booking rate?

Bookings ÷ auditions over a set period, as a percentage. Be consistent about what counts as an audition.

What’s a good booking rate?

There isn’t one universal number. Track your own trend. Context beats bragging rights.

Should I include callbacks in the booking rate?

No — booking rate is bookings over auditions. Track callback rate separately so you can see where the funnel breaks.
