Audition prep:Building the choices before you walk in.

Lines without homework is karaoke. Casting has already read the script. Your job is to walk in (or hit record) with research, a goal, and a sense of how the scene sits in the story — then stay loose enough to play.

This is the Audition Prep list from Actor Tips in the app. Keep notes on the job in the Auditions tab so the prep stays with the role, not in a separate Notes graveyard.

Read the credits before you learn the lines

Director, writers, producers, company, anyone attached. Tone lives there. A kitchen-sink BBC one-off and a glossy streamer procedural want different sizes of performance even on the same “worried mum” sides.

The more you know about the story, the less generic your choices. Five minutes on IMDb and the company’s last three titles is not procrastination. It is the first beat of the work.

What does this person actually want?

Get specific: what they want in the scene, why they want it, and what they will do to get it. Vague “they want love” does not give you behaviour. “They want her to stay in the room until she admits she called the police” does.

That engine is what the reader feels even when the dialogue is small talk.

You might not be the lead. Fine.

Know how this character supports the story and moves it forward. Are you the obstacle? The witness? The joke that lets the hero breathe? Play that function without turning into a signpost.

Supporting sides still need a full inner life. They do not need a secret bid for the title role in every glance.

Who are you talking to — including the ones who never appear?

How do you feel about the other people in the scene? Do they help or block the goal? Off-screen characters count: the partner in the other room, the boss on the phone, the kid you just dropped off.

Your reactions get cheaper when “the other person” is just a reader with a script. Give them a history, even if it is one sentence.

Borrow their objective for a minute

Look at the scene from the other characters’ goals too. If you know what they are fighting for, your listening changes. You stop waiting for your next line and start dealing with a person who wants something.

The join between beats

Nail the shifts — emotional and behavioural, big or tiny. A clean transition is what makes it feel lived-in instead of “now I am angry, now I am sad.” Find the thought that turns the corner, then play the new beat.

Do the opposite take

Do not lock one version of the scene because it felt clever in the kitchen. Try the opposite choice. Playful, colder, smaller, needier. The fresh one is often the take you would not have found by polishing take one.

Play it for the lens without going “presentational”

Picture how it reads on camera: eyelines, size in frame, where everyone sits. Film acting is not theatre projected smaller. It is thought that lands in the eyes, then the body follows.

For the tape version of this (light, wall, mic), use the self-tape checklist.

Watch it like they will

Record in-person prep and self-tapes. The camera shows the sniff, the sway, the eyeline that missed. Watch once as a reader, not as the person who just suffered the take. Then stop picking and go in.

Do the work. Not the booking.

Focus on the scene in front of you. When the outcome loosens its grip, the audition gets freer — and usually better. You cannot control the list. You can control homework, a goal, and a take that is alive.

Keep the prep with the job

A one-line note on the audition card (accent, relationship, the opposite take that worked) is enough. Log the job the day the sides arrive so this work has a home.
